Main findings

  • The cop was faster than every one of our riders. 20 km/h vs 10–16 km/h on the same filmed stretch (p = 0.004).M1
  • The gap is made in the turns, not on the last straight. The turn speed decides how a ride ends (p = 0.006). The simulation shows he left the turns at ~13 km/h vs our 6, and that turn friction alone moves the speed out of the tube from 12 to 18 km/h.M2S2L2
  • Our riders crawled because they rode on the stall point. Friction in the turns 0.54–0.62 against a stall at 0.58; the cop's was about 0.15.M3W3
  • To match the cop you must leave the tube at ≥ 18 km/h. No straight friction or posture fixes a slow exit: posture changes the last straight by < 2 % at equal speed.M4L1L4
  • Lying flat goes with fast rides, but posture alone does not explain the cop. Measured: lying flat 4.2 vs 2.7 km/h in the turns (p = 0.001) and 7.2 vs 13.3 s for the full descent (p = 0.003). Simulated: posture changes the whole-slide time by ±15 % with our friction, ~1 % with his.M5M10W2
  • Pushing harder helps, but not enough. Measured ρ = 0.46 (p = 0.05); simulated 16 km/h push still slower than the cop.M5W1
  • Not the suit, not body size. No clear group differences in the turns or over the full descent (taller riders slower, p = 0.04, only suggestive); the cop suit made nobody faster.M6M10M7
  • The cop's swing and twist come from speed and a relaxed body. The simulation reproduces his 82° roll at the tube mouth with no special technique; tone changes the twist, not the time.S2L3
  • The numbers hold up. Two sensors agree within 7 %; the simulation matches video it was not fitted to (within 0.1 s at 10.5 m); riders' own ratings rank them correctly (ρ = −0.80 with the full descent time).M8S1M9M11

Limits: one run per rider per outfit; the cop's numbers rest on two hand-read times from a 15-fps clip; posture was seen after the turns. M = measured, S/W/L = simulated (full-body physics, calibrated to the measurements).

Measured

M1

On the only stretch filmed for both, every rider was slower than the cop

Tube mouth → end lip (3.95 m): our fastest riders averaged 10–16 km/h, the cop 20 km/h (all 9 slower, p = 0.004).

Times read frame by frame on physical slide features (±0.035–0.07 s).

M2

How fast you take the turns decides how the ride ends

Stopped before the flat: 2.0 ± 0.5 km/h in the turns; stopped on the flat: 3.3 ± 0.7; shot off the end: 4.2 ± 0.7 (p = 0.006). The ones who shot off were also slipperier on the lower straight (friction 0.27 vs 0.48 and 0.59, p < 0.001).

Dots = runs, black = mean ± SD. Ending seen on the landing camera.

M3

Everyone rode on the stall point

On a 30° slope, friction 0.58 cancels gravity. Our riders had 0.54–0.62 in the turns, where a small change in friction is a big change in speed.

Curves = slide model for two push-off speeds; shaded = our riders.

M4

Matching the cop needs speed out of the tube

Every point on the gold line reproduces the cop's 0.70 s. Our riders all sit to its left: even the slipperiest came out of the tube 3–4 km/h too slowly. The "wannabe cop" (rain suit) is close to the cop.

Blue = our 9 riders who shot off, ±1σ (point model fitted to logger + video).

M5

Lying flat went with faster turns; push-off helped a little

Lying flat: 4.2 km/h in the turns vs 2.7 sitting up (p = 0.001); 6 of 8 shot off vs 1 of 11 (p = 0.006). Push-off: ρ = 0.46, p = 0.05.

Posture coded on the straight, after the turns: slow riders may also sit up because they are slow.

M6

Clothes and body size: no clear difference

Sex, age, weight, height, estimated contact area and pressure: p = 0.19–0.82. Jeans slower (p = 0.02), only suggestive with 8 comparisons.

Bars = mean ± SD, normal-clothes runs only. Group averages only.

M10

The same comparison for the full descent time

Lying flat in the trough: 7.2 s median from the top to the end vs 13.3 s sitting up (p = 0.003, conclusive even with 8 tests). Weight, contact area and pressure: no difference. Taller riders were slower (8.6 vs 16.9 s, p = 0.04) and jeans slower (17.0 vs 8.6 s, p = 0.09): suggestive only.

Normal clothes, Mann-Whitney. Push-off is fitted and shares the launch time with the descent, so its difference is partly built in.

M11

What else goes with a fast full descent?

How fast it felt matches the real descent best (ρ = −0.80, p < 0.001). Fast rides knocked against the tube more (ρ = −0.51, p = 0.03): a consequence of speed rather than a cause. Weight, height, contact, pressure and body angle in the turns: nothing clear.

Grey = parts of the descent or of its fit, which correlate by construction. 12 tests: only p < 0.004 is conclusive.

M7

The cop suit did not make us faster

Of three riders who rode in both, P04 was faster in the suit, P05 slower, P06 the same.

One run each; the suit was always the second run.

What the simulation adds

The cop was only filmed for his last 4 m, and in our data speed, posture and friction move together. The full-body simulation, calibrated to each run, separates them and fills in what no camera saw.

S1

Validation: the simulation reproduces video it was not fitted to

Friction on the straight was fitted only to the 11.05 → 12.4 m time. The earlier video gates are an independent test: at 10.5 m the sim is within 0.03–0.11 s. From 9.5 m it covers the stretch about 0.3 s quicker than the real rider, so it slightly overestimates our riders' speed out of the tube. The real gap to the cop is, if anything, larger.

Full-body physics (Rapier), turn friction fitted to each rider's logger turn time, synchronised at 11.05 m. Both riders must leave the lip, as on the video; Stef then fits the window less well (0.26 s RMS vs 0.015 s for Angel).

S2

What nobody filmed: the cop's turns

His clip starts at the tube mouth. Calibrated to his 0.70 s, the simulation says he left the turns at about 13 km/h (ours 6) and came out of the tube at 18 (ours 10–12). Relaxed, at that speed, his body rolls 82° at the mouth, as on his video; our riders roll under 10°.

Cop: one friction for the whole slide (0.15) with a limp body; riders: 0.41 in the turns.

How much to trust the numbers

M9

Reading the statistics

  • p-value: how likely a difference this big would be by pure chance if there were really no difference. p = 0.004 means about 4 in 1000. Below 0.05 is the usual bar; with many comparisons we ask for less.
  • Kruskal–Wallis: compares three or more groups (here: stopped before the flat / on the flat / shot off). It ranks all riders from slowest to fastest and asks whether one group sits higher in the ranking, so a single extreme rider cannot drive the result. Suits small groups like ours.
  • Mann–Whitney: the same idea for two groups (lying flat vs sitting up).
  • Wilcoxon: compares a set of riders with one fixed value (the cop's 20 km/h), again by ranks. All 9 of our riders were slower: by chance alone that happens with p = 0.004.
  • Fisher's exact test: for counts, e.g. 6 of 8 lying flat shot off vs 1 of 11 sitting up.
  • Spearman ρ: how well two rankings agree, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (identical).
