So how did the cop go so fast?

  1. He kept his speed through the turns. Everyone lost speed there; the gap to the cop is made in the turns (sections 1–2).
  2. The best explanation we found is posture: riders lying flat were faster and more often shot off the end, and the cop (and the "wannabe cop") rode stretched out flat (section 3).
  3. Not the suit, and not body size. Push-off helps a little.

Limits: one run per rider per outfit; the cop's numbers rest on two hand-read times from a 15-fps clip; posture was seen after the turns. A same-rider posture test (flat vs sitting up, alternating order) would settle cause vs symptom.

1 · Where the speed goes

How fast you take the turns decides how the ride ends.

Riders who stopped before the flat crawled through the turns at 2.0 ± 0.5 km/h, those who stopped on the flat at 3.3 ± 0.7, and those who shot off the end at 4.2 ± 0.7 (p = 0.006). The ones who shot off were also much slipperier on the lower straight (friction 0.27 vs 0.48 and 0.59, p < 0.001).

Dots = runs, black = mean ± SD. Ending seen on the landing camera.

Everyone sat on a knife-edge: the stall point.

On a 30° slope, friction 0.58 exactly cancels gravity. All our riders had 0.54–0.62 in the turns, where the curve is steepest: a tiny change in friction or push-off is a big change in speed. The cop's friction (≈ 0.27) is far to the left.

Curves = the slide model for two push-off speeds; the shaded band = our riders.

2 · Us vs the cop

On the only stretch filmed for both, every rider was slower than the cop.

From the tube mouth to the end lip (3.95 m) our fastest riders averaged 10–16 km/h; the cop 20 km/h (all 9 slower, p = 0.004).

Times read frame by frame on physical slide features; every reading is in results/gate_checks.

The cop came out of the tube much faster.

Our fastest rider (fitted to logger + video) left the tube at ~13 km/h. To cover the last 3.95 m in 0.70 s the cop had to leave it at 17–22 km/h, whatever his friction there.

Gold lines = two of the many speed/friction pairs that fit his clip.

Matching the cop needs speed out of the tube, not just a slippery straight.

Every combination on the gold line reproduces the cop's 0.70 s. Our riders (blue) all sit far to its left: even the slipperiest came out of the tube 3–4 km/h too slowly. The "wannabe cop" (rain suit, lying like the cop) is close to him.

Blue dots = our 9 riders who shot off, ±1σ.

The fix is in the turns: posture on the straight barely helps.

Rolling onto the side or moulding into the trough saves ~0.02 s; even the lowest possible friction saves 0.06 s. Only leaving the tube ~5 km/h faster reaches the cop's 0.70 s — and that speed has to be kept through the turns.

Slide model applied to our fastest rider.

3 · What made a rider fast

Posture is the strongest factor we found; push-off helps only a little.

Riders lying flat on their back did 4.2 km/h in the turns vs 2.7 sitting up (p = 0.001), and 6 of 8 shot off the end vs 1 of 11 (p = 0.006) — like the cop, who went stretched out flat. A harder push-off helped only slightly (p = 0.05).

Posture coded on the straight, after the turns: slow riders may also sit up because they are slow.

Clothes and body size made no clear difference; jeans were slowest.

Sex, age, drinks, weight, height, estimated contact area and pressure: p = 0.19–0.82. Jeans were slower (p = 0.02), suggestive only: with 8 comparisons, p < 0.006 would be needed.

Bars = mean ± SD, normal-clothes runs only. Only group averages shown.

The cop suit did not make us faster.

Of the three riders who rode in both, P04 was faster in the suit, P05 slower and P06 the same. Angel and Stef's suit runs (day 1) were also far from the cop.

One run each; the suit was always the second run.

4 · How much to trust the numbers

Two independent sensors give the same turn speeds.

The waist logger and the chest GoPro's own gyro timed every right turn separately; they agree within 7 % (median). Rider-to-rider differences (about ±50 %) are real, not noise.

On the line = perfect agreement; shaded = ±10 %.

Riders knew how fast they were.

Their own rating (×10 = % of the cop) matched the measured speed on the filmed stretch within a few points (p = 0.15), and put everyone in the right order (rank correlation 0.78, p < 0.001).
